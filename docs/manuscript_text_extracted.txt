
Integrated brain–body physiological monitoring from a smart earbud

Abstract: Continuous monitoring of interacting physiological systems is central to digital health but remains challenging because wearable devices typically capture isolated signals from distributed body locations. The human ear provides a unique anatomical site where neural, cardiovascular, respiratory, and thermoregulatory signals converge, enabling integrated physiological monitoring from a single interface. Here we present EarSense, a smart earbud that integrates adaptive deep ear-canal electrophysiology enabled by shape-memory actuators and hydrogel electrodes with optical, thermal, electrodermal, and inertial sensing modalities. EarSense enables simultaneous acquisition of neural activity, reconstructed cardiac electrophysiology, vascular dynamics, respiration patterns, core body temperature, electrodermal activity, and body motion from a single ear-worn device. In human studies, EarSense supports continuous monitoring during daily activities and sleep, enabling quantitative assessment of affective states, automated sleep staging with 89.9% agreement with polysomnography, and prediction of next-day cognitive performance. These results establish the ear as a practical interface for integrated brain–body physiological monitoring in real-world settings.
Main Text
Continuous physiological monitoring in daily life is a central goal of digital health and personalized medicine (1-5). Wearable technologies have advanced this vision by enabling continuous measurement of vital signs outside clinical environments (6-10). However, human physiology arises from tightly coupling among neural, cardiovascular, respiratory, thermoregulatory, and sympathetic systems (11). Most wearable devices monitor only a limited subset of these signals from isolated body locations—for example, wrist-based photoplethysmography (PPG) for heart rate, chest patches for electrocardiography (ECG), or scalp electrodes for electroencephalography (EEG). As a result, current systems provide fragmented physiological information and fail to capture coordinated brain–body dynamics underlying health, behavior, and cognitive performance.
Achieving integrated monitoring of multiple physiological systems with wearables remains challenging. Key physiological signals originate from spatially distributed organs and tissues and are traditionally measured at different anatomical locations. Although combining multiple devices can in principle provide a more complete picture of physiological state (12), distributed sensing increases system complexity, introduces synchronization and calibration challenges, and burdens users.  Moreover, neural monitoring—essential for understanding brain–body regulation—remains difficult to achieve at high fidelity in wearable systems. Conventional EEG requires bulky hardware or extensive preparation, whereas wearable EEG often relies on dry or shallow electrodes with unstable interfaces, causing high impedance, motion artifacts, and reduced signal fidelity during long-term monitoring (13-15). The limited availability of near-brain sensing sites further complicates compact integration of EEG with other physiological sensors, so most systems operate in isolation and fail to capture integrated brain–body physiology (16, 17). 
The human ear offers a promising anatomical site for addressing these challenges. Several physiological pathways converge within the auricular region, enabling access to signals originating from multiple organ systems (Fig. 1A and text S1). The ear canal lies in close proximity to the temporal cortex, allowing detection of neural activity using in-ear electrophysiology (18). Cardiac electrical signals propagate throughout the body via volume conduction and can also be detected near the ear (19, 20). The auricular vasculature preserves pulsatile and respiration-modulated blood flow signatures that can be measured optically (21), while the tympanic membrane provides a well-established proxy for core body temperature (CBT) (22). In addition, the postauricular skin contains eccrine sweat glands that enable electrodermal monitoring of sympathetic activity. Together, these features make the ear a compact and protected site where neural, cardiovascular, respiratory, thermal, and sympathetic signals intersect, enabling integrated physiological monitoring from a single wearable interface.
Here we present EarSense, a smart earbud that enables multimodal monitoring of brain and peripheral physiology (Fig. 1A). EarSense integrates adaptive deep ear-canal electrophysiology enabled by shape-memory actuators and hydrogel electrodes with optical, thermal, electrodermal, and inertial sensing modalities (Fig. 1, B and C). The actuator–hydrogel interface establishes stable, low-impedance contact within the ear canal while maintaining gentle mechanical coupling, enabling high-fidelity electrophysiological recording during long-term wear. This configuration enables simultaneous acquisition of neural activity via EEG, machine-learning (ML)–reconstructed ECG, vascular and respiration dynamics via PPG, CBT, electrodermal activity via galvanic skin response (GSR), and body motion from a single ear-worn device. 
Using this integrated sensing approach, we demonstrate continuous multimodal physiological monitoring during daily activities and sleep (Fig. 1, D and E, and fig. S1). In human studies, EarSense captures coordinated brain–body signals that enable affective state assessment and automated sleep staging. Leveraging ML algorithms, EarSense achieves quantitative assessment of affective state levels (R² > 0.9), 89.9% agreement with polysomnography (PSG) for sleep staging, and accurate prediction of next-day cognitive performance (R² = 0.88) (Fig. 1F). Together, these results establish the ear as a practical interface for integrated brain–body monitoring and highlight ear-worn devices as a scalable approach for real-world assessment of complex physiological states.
Ear-worn multimodal sensing architecture
To realize such integrated sensing, we designed an anatomically adaptive ear-worn system that combines deep ear-canal electrophysiology with multimodal physiological sensing. EarSense comprises three primary components: a compact tip assembly for electrophysiology and tympanic CBT sensing; a central flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) for signal acquisition and wireless communication; and an adaptive enclosure with a soft hook for device stabilization, peripheral sensing, and power supply housing (Fig. 1, A to C). 
The tip assembly contains a four-actuator hydrogel array forming deep ear-canal electrophysiological interface and protrudes from a soft elastic ear tip. This multi-actuator architecture and compliant substrate enable stable and conformal electrode–skin contact inside the ear canal (Fig. 1C and fig. S2). The actuators surround a central holder containing an infrared (IR) sensor for noncontact tympanic CBT measurement. 
All sensing and system components are distributed across the main enclosure and ear hook, interfacing with the FPCB through flexible interconnects or compact wiring (Fig. 1B). A PPG sensor is oriented toward the vascular-rich concha to capture pulsatile and respiration-modulated vascular signals. GSR electrodes positioned on the postauricular skin enable electrodermal sensing while minimizing electrical crosstalk between electrophysiological recordings. Biopotential reference (Ref) and ground (GND) electrodes are placed near the mastoid to minimize neural and muscular interference. Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries are housed in the distal hook and support docking-based charging. The complete system weighs less than 23 g, supporting comfortable long-term wear (Fig. 1, D and E).
Adaptive actuator-hydrogel interface for deep ear-canal electrophysiology
Ear EEG is a promising approach for wearable neural monitoring because of the ear’s proximity to brain, reduced hair-related impedance variability, and socially acceptable form factor. However, existing ear-EEG systems typically use shallow ear-canal (15, 17) or postauricular locations (23), and rely on dry (24) or short-lived electrodes (16), resulting in unstable contact, elevated impedance, motion sensitivity, and degraded signal fidelity.
To address these challenges, EarSense uses an actuator-hydrogel interface that establishes stable low-impedance contact at deep ear-canal locations (Fig. 2A). This actuator features a bilayer of prestretched elastic polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and nickel–titanium (NiTi) shape memory alloy (SMA), enabling reversible actuation (Fig. 2B). At low temperatures, the compliant martensitic SMA permits elastomer-driven bending, whereas heating induces transformation toward the stiffer austenitic phase and drives recovery toward a straighter configuration (text S2 and fig. S3). 
To optimize in-ear operation and skin-safe heating, the SMA was thermally aged to tune its transformation behavior. Aging increases martensitic-related character and reduces stiffness at room temperature and sharpens thermal recovery through Ni₄Ti₃ precipitation and associated coherency strains (figs. S4 to S6 and text S3) (25, 26). With aged SMA and optimized PDMS thickness and pre-stretch, the actuator showed nearly doubled bending curvature and recovery rates exceeding 96% (figs. S7 and S8). Although the free-state transformation temperatures exceed  skin-safe threshold (~45 °C), the bilayer architecture induced stress-assisted reverse transformation (27) (text S4), enabling full recovery at ~45 °C within 1 min (Fig. 2B, fig. S9, and movie S1).
The actuator showed no measurable degradation over 800 heating–cooling cycles (Fig. 2C and fig. S10), supported a 0.2-g load without curvature loss (fig. S11 and movie S2), and fully recovered after rolling, folding, and twisting (fig. S12 and movie S3). A four-actuator array was constructed to provide stable multipoint contact and multichannel recording in the ear canal (Fig. 2D). At physiological temperature (~37 °C), the array remains in its default open configuration, minimizing power consumption. Brief heating to 45 °C induces transition to a closed state for insertion or retraction, followed by reopening after cooling (fig. S9), with synchronized actuation across all units (fig. S13 and movie S4). The array spans an equivalent diameter from ~4 mm (closed) to ~10 mm (open), encompassing the average human ear-canal diameter (~7 mm) and enabling easy insertion and reliable post-insertion contact (Fig. 2, D and E and movie S5). Contact pressure in the open state remained below the skin perception threshold (~20 kPa) (16), supporting comfortable long-term wear (Fig. 2F).
To ensure low-impedance coupling, a water-retentive ionically conductive acrylamide-based  hydrogel (WR-IC-PAAm) was integrated onto the actuator tips by in situ interfacial bonding (Fig. 2, A and G and figs. S14 and S15) (28-31). Lithium chloride in the glycerol–water cosolvent enhances hydration (32, 33) and ionic conductivity (34) without compromising softness (figs. S16 to S18). This interface yielded a contact impedance of ~35 kΩ at 50 Hz, comparable to commercial Ag/AgCl semi-dry and saline wet electrodes and substantially lower than bare Au dry electrodes (Fig. 2H and fig. S19). 
This stabilized interface markedly improves deep ear-canal recording quality. Under alpha modulation (AM), steady-state visually evoked potentials (SSVEP), and auditory steady-state responses (ASSR) paradigms, hydrogel-enabled recordings exhibit clear neural oscillations together with discernible cardiac signatures within the same biopotential traces (fig. S20). Neural recording quality is reflected by an AM ratio of 3.75 and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) values of 2.94 dB (AM), 9.13 dB (SSVEP), and 6.72 dB (ASSR), all substantially higher than those obtained using bare Au electrodes (fig. S21). Distinct cardiac signatures were preserved in hydrogel-enabled recordings but were largely absent with dry electrodes (fig. S20).
The hydrogel also maintains long-term stability. It remained fully hydrated at 25 °C and retained ~60% of its initial water content at 37 °C after one week (fig. S22), resulting in only a modest increase to ~50 kΩ at 37 °C (Fig. S23). Week-long daily recordings using the same hydrogel electrodes exhibited stable alpha peaks, AM ratios, SNRs (Fig. 2I and fig. S24), and preserved cardiac signatures (fig. S25).  By contrast, commercial gels rapidly dehydrated and showed pronounced impedance increases and signal degradation within one day (Fig. 2H and figs. S26 to S39).
Deep ear-canal recordings further enhance artifact tolerance and biopotential fidelity. Compared with shallow ear-tip recordings, actuator-enabled deep ear-canal recordings largely suppressed Electrooculogram (EOG) transients (fig. S30) and remained resilient to facial and head motions, under which shallow placements degraded markedly (Fig. 2J and fig. S31). Even at low contact pressure (1.5 kPa), electrode–skin impedance remained low (~40 kΩ) and high-quality neural recordings were preserved (fig. S33). Alpha-modulation recordings demonstrate consistent oscillatory and cardiac signatures across 1.5 –10 kPa (figs. S34 and S35), indicating robustness to inter-individual anatomical variability. Power spectral density (PSD) spectrograms show clear alpha peaks with low baseline noise at all pressures, whereas shallow ear-tip electrodes produce noisy spectra (Fig. 2K). Histological analysis in rats revealed no detectable tissue damage or inflammation after 2 weeks of actuator contact (fig. S36), supporting long-term wear suitability. Finally, recordings from the array exhibit excellent inter-channel uniformity (fig. S37).
Reconstruction of full ECG waveforms from ear-canal composite biopotentials
Cardiac electrical activity propagates throughout the body via volume conduction and can therefore be detected near the ear. However, in conventional ear-EEG systems, cardiac signals are typically attenuated and obscured by low SNR and unstable interfaces. By contrast, the high-fidelity deep ear-canal recordings enabled by the actuator–hydrogel interface preserve distinct cardiac signatures, particularly R peaks (fig. S20).
Leveraging these preserved cardiac components, we developed an attention-augmented autoencoder network (ECGnet) to reconstruct full ECG waveforms from deep ear-canal signals (Fig. 2L and fig. S38). ECGnet extracts local morphological features and integrates long-range temporal dependencies across cardiac cycles, with ECG-aware training constraints in both the time and frequency domains, to recover coherent, physiologically meaningful P–QRS–T morphology.
The reconstructed ECG showed high agreement with reference arm ECG (Fig. 2M), with cosine similarity and Pearson correlation coefficients (r) of 0.92 (fig. S39A), a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.03 mV, and a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.003 mV2 (fig. S39B). Cardiac-related spectral components were clearly recovered but were largely absent in the original composite biopotential (fig. S40). R-to-R intervals (RRI) and heart rate (HR) trajectories derived from reconstructed ECG showed near-perfect agreement with reference ECG (R² = 0.998) (Fig. 2N and fig. S41). 
Clinically relevant ECG metrics could also be extracted reliably from the reconstructed signals. Bland–Altman analyses demonstrated strong agreement across multiple parameters, including JT interval, diastolic interval, and QT-to-RRI ratio (Fig. 2, O and P; fig. S42). Compared with prior approaches that rely on contralateral electrodes or provide only limited R-peak detection, this method enables reconstruction of complete, clinically meaningful cardiac waveforms from a single ear-canal recording (table S1). These results show that a single ear-canal biopotential measurement can support simultaneous neural monitoring and full ECG reconstruction. 
System integration and multimodal physiological sensing
EarSense integrates electrophysiological, optical, thermal, electrodermal, and inertial sensing within a compact earbud through a space-efficient FPCB design, optimized layout, and integrated electronics (Fig. 3A). The central FPCB incorporates the biopotential front-end, PPG, IR thermometer, inertial measurement unit (IMU), GSR interface, and microcontroller with Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) communication (Fig. 3, B and C; fig. S43). Dedicated power management coordinates actuator heating, GSR operation, and system regulation for reliable multimodal operation. Actuator heating requires ~3 W of power but is only needed before wearing and completes within 1 min. Multimodal sensing with BLE transmission consumes less than 0.24 W, enabling over 9 h of continuous operation and up to 24 h under optimized interval streaming using two 300 mAh batteries (fig. S44).
From ear PPG, pulse waveforms, respiration patterns, and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were extracted using tailored signal processing (Fig. 3D and fig. S45), with high SNRs for pulse (15.66 dB) and respiration signals (23.74 dB) (fig. S46), enabling accurate measurement of HR, respiration rate (RR), and SpO₂ with strong agreement to an FDA-cleared pulse oximeter (fig. S47). Tympanic temperature closely reflects CBT (Fig. 3E) and is less sensitive to environmental fluctuations than skin temperature. A noncontact IR sensor enables stable CBT measurement without the baseline drift common to contact-based thermistors (fig. S48). GSR electrodes positioned along the ear hook measure postauricular skin conductance (Fig. 3F) while minimizing crosstalk during concurrent electrophysiology recording (fig. S49).
To evaluate real-world performance, we conducted a continuous monitoring study in a healthy participant across 24-hour daily activities and sleep (Fig. 3G). During exercise, HR, RR, respiration amplitude (RA), GSR, and CBT increased markedly and returned to baseline after activity cessation. Sleep states were identifiable from EEG spectral transitions and stage-specific oscillations. Across sleep, HR decreased with parasympathetic dominance and reduced metabolic demand, while CBT followed its circadian trajectory, reaching an early-morning nadir before rising toward wakefulness. These results demonstrate that EarSense enables stable continuous multimodal monitoring in naturalistic settings, supporting integrated assessment of physiological, cognitive, and behavioral states. 
Affective state evaluation using EarSense
EarSense was applied to monitor human affective states that arise from coordinated brain–body physiological responses (Fig. 4A) (35). Controlled experiments were conducted in 15 healthy participants under four affective conditions: relaxation, cold pressor test (CPT), virtual reality (VR) challenge, and recovery (table S2). Multimodal physiological signals were continuously recorded using EarSense (Fig. 4B and 4C), while subjective affective states were assessed using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) (36) and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form Y (STAI-Y) (37), administered after each condition (table S3). 
During CPT, participants immersed one hand in ice water for 5 min, inducing nociceptive stress and sympathetic activation, with increased negative affect (NA) and state anxiety levels (fig. S50, A to C). This condition produced rapid cardiorespiratory and electrodermal responses, including changes in HR, heart rate variability (HRV), SpO₂, RA, and GSR (Fig. 4B). During the VR challenge, participants underwent a 10-min immersive roller-coaster simulation that elicited heightened emotional arousal and anticipatory engagement, resulting in increased positive affect (PA), NA and STAI scores (fig. S50, D to E), accompanied by neural spectral redistribution and cardiorespiratory modulation (Fig. 4C). Relaxation and recovery periods (10 min each) were defined immediately before and after CPT or VR exposure.
To quantify affective states, we implemented a supervised ML pipeline that maps physiological features to both discrete affective conditions and questionnaire-derived scores (Fig. 4D). EarSense captures characteristic brain–body dynamics across affective states, exemplified by increased EEG beta-band power (Fig. 4E) and decreased ECG-derived root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) (Fig. 4F) during CPT and VR exposure, consistent with elevated cortical arousal and reduced vagal tone. 
Using multimodal physiological features, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) classifier achieved the best performance, distinguishing the four affective states with 99.2% cross-validated accuracy (Fig. 4G and fig. S51) and a macro-F1 score of 0.992 (fig. S52). The multimodal data further enabled quantitative prediction of continuous affective scores. An LGBM regression model accurately predicted PA, NA, and STAI scores, with R² ≈ 0.91 and MAE ≈ 1–2 across targets (Fig. 4H to 4J; figs. S53 to S55). These results indicate that EarSense captures graded affective dynamics rather than merely distinguishing experimental conditions.
Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) revealed that the most influential predictors span complementary neural, autonomic cardiovascular–respiratory, and motion domains, (Fig. 4K and fig. S56). reflecting coordinated brain–body responses to stress. The decision plot further illustrates how these multimodal signals collectively determine affective state predictions (Fig. 4L). Together, these results demonstrate that accurate affect inference arises from integrated physiological dynamics.
Sleep monitoring and functional and cognitive performance evaluation using EarSense
Sleep involves coordinated neural and peripheral regulation, making integrated brain–body monitoring essential for comprehensive sleep assessment. However, gold-standard PSG requires complex laboratory instrumentation, whereas most wearables rely on limited peripheral proxies.
EarSense’s multimodal brain–body sensing enables naturalistic sleep monitoring and facilitates linking overnight physiological dynamics to next-day functional and cognitive performance (Fig. 5A). Overnight studies were conducted in 27 participants (20 healthy and 7 patients with sleep disorders) wearing EarSense, including 6 patients who also underwent simultaneous PSG monitoring (Fig. 5B and table S4). Subjective outcomes—including daytime sleepiness, sleep quality, and functional status—were assessed using Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Functional Outcomes of Sleep Questionnaire (FOSQ), administered before and after sleep. Objective vigilance was further evaluated using a 10-min psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) (table S5). 
EarSense continuously captured coordinated physiological dynamics across PSG-defined sleep stages (Fig. 5C). Consistent with canonical sleep physiology, HR and CBT declined overnight to an early-morning nadir, while respiratory stabilized and HRV remained relatively elevated during non–rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep. GSR stayed generally low with transient arousal-associated fluctuations, and body motion was minimal except during brief awakenings. EEG patterns recorded by EarSense exhibited strong agreement with PSG across sleep stages, with spectrograms recapitulated PSG-derived stage transitions and oscillatory dynamics, yielding an overall similarity of Pearson r = 0.83 and strong concordance across canonical bands (delta–beta; r ≈ 0.8–0.9) (Fig. 5, D and E and fig. S57). Sleep spindle events detected by EarSense also aligned closely with PSG detections, achieving an F1 score of 0.54, comparable to inter-expert agreement in spindle scoring (38, 39) (Fig. 5F and table S6).
To quantify sleep stages, we developed a temporal-context deep convolutional neural network integrating multimodal inputs across neighboring epochs to capture sleep dynamics. A cascade classifier with probabilistic refinement of the ambiguous N1 transition stage further improves stage discrimination (fig. S58). Accordingly, EarSense achieved automated sleep staging with 89.9% agreement and a Cohen’s κ of 0.88 with PSG-based ground truth across five stages (Fig. 5G). The predicted hypnogram of a representative patient with sleep disorders closely tracked PSG-derived overnight stage transitions, reaching 91.7% agreement with a Cohen’s κ of 0.88 (Fig. 5H). This performance places EarSense among the highest-performing wearable sleep monitoring systems, surpassing most commercial and laboratory-developed systems that rely on limited modalities or peripheral proxies (table S7).
Beyond sleep staging, EarSense enables prediction of next-day functional and cognitive outcomes from overnight physiological dynamics. Multinomial logistic regression models trained on EarSense-derived sleep features accurately predicted subjective outcome levels assessed via questionnaires, including ESS (40, 41), PSQI (42, 43), and FOSQ (44), achieving agreements of 85.2%, 96.3%, and 96.3%, respectively (Fig. 5, I to K), with misclassifications predominantly confined to adjacent severity levels and remaining within ±1 level of the ground truth in 96.3–100% of cases (fig. S59). A multilayer perceptron regressor further quantitatively predicted the slowest 10% reaction time on the PVT, a sleep-sensitive index of cognitive performance, with R² = 0.88 and MAE = 22.9 ms (Fig. 5L). Bland–Altman and residual analyses confirming strong agreement and minimal systematic bias (fig. S60). 
User surveys indicated minimal side effects during overnight use, with a mean wearing comfort score of 7.93/10 and a willingness-to-replace PSG score of 9.18/10 (table S8). Together, these results demonstrate that EarSense enables accurate, continuous sleep monitoring and links physiological dynamics to next-day functional and cognitive outcomes, highlighting its potential as a scalable platform for real-world assessment of sleep and cognitive performance.
Conclusions
We present a smart earbud that enables integrated multimodal sensing of coordinated physiological systems, including neural, cardiovascular, respiratory, thermoregulatory, sympathetic, and motion dynamics. By leveraging the ear as a physiological convergence site, EarSense captures brain–body dynamics through electrophysiological, optical, thermal, electrodermal, and inertial sensing. A deep ear-canal electrophysiology interface, enabled by adaptive shape-memory actuators and water-retentive ionically conductive hydrogel electrodes, establishes stable low-impedance contact for high-fidelity composite biopotential acquisition, enabling simultaneous EEG monitoring and reconstruction of clinically meaningful ECG waveforms. 
Human studies show that EarSense supports continuous multimodal monitoring during daily activities and sleep, enabling quantitative assessment of affective states, automated sleep staging, and prediction of next-day functional and cognitive performance. Together, these results establish the ear as a practical interface for integrated physiological monitoring and highlight the potential of ear-worn devices for unobtrusive assessment of complex physiological states in real-world environments.

Materials and Methods
Materials
Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Sylgard 184) was purchased from Dow Inc. Nickel–titanium (NiTi) shape memory alloy was obtained from Luoyang Shengsheng Metal Materials Co., Ltd. Polyimide (PI) films (12.5 μm thick) were purchased from American Durafilm Co., Inc. Rigid and soft 3D-printing resins were purchased from Anycubic Technology Co., Ltd (Standard Resin, clear) and 3DMaterials Co., Ltd (Superflex, clear), respectively. Acrylamide, N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide, ammonium persulfate, N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine, and 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Acetic acid, acetone, ethanol, and isopropanol were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.
Methods
Fabrication and assembly of EarSense
Fabrication of the Au heater and Au electrode. Au heater and biopotential electrodes were fabricated on 12.5-μm-thick PI substrates using photolithography and lift-off processes. After substrate cleaning with acetone and isopropanol, photoresist patterns were defined, followed by electron-beam evaporation of Ti/Au (5/100 nm) at 0.5 and 1.0 Å s⁻¹, respectively. Lift-off was performed in Remover PG (Kayaku Advanced Materials), followed by rinsing with IPA and drying under nitrogen.
Thermal aging of the NiTi SMA. NiTi SMA sheets were clamped between glass slides and thermally aged at 450 °C for 8 h under external loading to maintain a straight geometry, followed by quenching in room-temperature water.
Synthesis of the WR-IC-PAAm hydrogel. WR-IC-PAAm hydrogel was prepared from a degassed precursor containing AAm (23 wt%), MBAA (0.051 wt%), and APS (0.043 wt%), followed by addition of TEMED (0.0575 wt%) to initiate polymerization. The precursor was cast in a PDMS mold and cured at 60 °C for 15 min. To strengthen hydrogel–Au adhesion, Au substrates were solvent-cleaned, oxygen-plasma treated for 10 min, and functionalized with 2 wt% TMSPMA in aqueous solution (pH 3.5) for 2 h before in situ hydrogel curing. The resulting hydeogel was then equilibrated for 6 h in 3 M LiCl solution containing glycerol/water (66.6/33.3 vol%) (figs. S15 and S16).
Fabrication of the actuator-hydrogel interface. As shown in fig. S14, uncured PDMS (10:1 prepolymer:crosslinker) was spin-coated on glass, cured at 80 °C for 2 h, peeled off, and laser-cut into 5 × 30 mm strips. The strips were prestretched and fixed on a second glass substrate. After oxygen-plasma treatment of the prestretched PDMS and NiTi SMA, an ultrathin layer of uncured PDMS was applied as an adhesive, and the SMA strip was laminated onto the PDMS and cured at 80 °C for 2 h. A thin-film Au/PI heater was attached to the SMA side, and a thin-film Au/PI biopotential electrode was bonded to the PDMS side. After laser release, the hydrogel electrode was formed in situ on the Au electrode using a 3D-printed mold.
Assembly of EarSense. The enclosure was designed in SolidWorks and fabricated by stereolithography (Mars 3 Pro, Elegoo) using rigid and soft resins. A PDMS ear tip was molded using 3D-printed molds. Circular stainless-steel electrodes serving as GSR, reference, and ground contacts, together with two parallel-connected lithium-ion batteries, were integrated into the ear hook and connected to the central FPCB. Within the main enclosure, charging electrodes, metal plates, and magnetic switches were integrated for aligned charging and power control, after which the central FPCB was installed. For the tip module, a two-part 3D-printed holder was used to mount the IR thermometer and its FPCB extension. Stainless-steel electrodes on the inner surfaces of the ear-tip holder served as biopotential conduction pathways, whereas electrodes on the outer surfaces of the temperature holder provided electrical interconnects to heaters. All leads were routed to the main circuit, and the temperature holder, actuator support, and ear-tip holder were assembled into an integrated tip module. The device was then wired, enclosed, and sealed to complete EarSense (Fig. 1C, Fig. 3A, and fig. S2).
Mechanical and materials characterization
Mechanical characterization. Tensile tests of WR-IC-PAAm hydrogels with varying LiCl contents were characterized with a force gauge (M5-50, Mark-10) using dog-bone samples (fig. S18). Interfacial adhesion between the hydrogel and Au substrate was evaluated by a standard 180° peel test (ASTM F2256) (fig. S15). The elastic modulus of the SMA was measured by a cantilever probing test using a strain gauge–based force sensor (HBTLM619BL, Haibo Sensor) coupled to an HX711 ADC module (SparkFun), with displacement controlled by a tensile testing machine (fig. S6A). Actuator contact pressure was characterized using the same force sensor in a cantilever configuration with a 2.5 V DC bias applied to the integrated Au heater; restoring force during cooling-driven recovery was recorded at adjustable vertical gaps to emulate ear canals of different diameters.
SMA phase and crystalline structure characterization. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of original and thermally aged NiTi SMA samples was performed on a DSC 25 (TA Instruments). Samples were cooled from room temperature to 0 °C at 1 °C min⁻¹, heated to 80 °C at the same rate, and cooled back to 0 °C; the cycle was repeated three times while heat flow was recorded (fig. S4). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα₁ radiation (50 kV, 40 mA) (fig. S5).
Hydrogel water retention characterization. Water retention abilities of WR-IC-PAAm hydrogel and commercial electrodes, including a saline wet gel (Parker Signa Gel) and an Ag/AgCl semi-dry electrode (3M Red DotTM 2560), were tested in parallel and stored at 25 or 37 °C and 50% relative humidity. For mass measurements, the saline gel was placed on weighing paper, and the Ag/AgCl gel was removed from the patch backing and metal connector. Remaining mass was recorded over 24 h and over 7 days to evaluate water retention (figs. S16 and S22).
In vivo tissue damage under long-term actuator contact
An in vivo rat model was used under protocols approved by the California Institute of Technology Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. IA23-1800). Twelve-week-old male Long–Evans rats wore a custom jacket-mounted 3D-printed holder that maintained continuous actuator tip contact with the dorsal skin. After 7 or 14 days, skin tissues were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological analysis. 
Electronic system design and characterization
The EarSense electronic system was implemented on a four-layer FPCB (34.3 mm × 15.15 mm × 1 mm) designed in Eagle CAD and powered by two lithium-ion batteries (Lanzhd 801525, 300 mAh each), providing 3.7 V and a total capacity of 600 mAh. Charging was enabled through exposed contact pads interfacing with a custom dock, while magnetic reed switches (MK24-B-3-OE, Standex-Meder Electronics) disconnected the main PCB during charging. Power management used a boost converter (TPS63070RNMR, Texas Instruments) followed by low-dropout regulators (ADM7172CP, Analog Devices) to generate stabilized 5 V and 3.3 V rails. Electrophysiological signals were acquired using an eight-channel analog front-end (ADS1299IPAG, Texas Instruments). PPG, inertial motion, and tympanic temperature were measured using a MAXM86161 optical sensor (Analog Devices), a BMI270 accelerometer (Bosch Sensortec), and an MLX90632SLD infrared sensor (Melexis Technologies), respectively. All sensing modules interfaced with an ESP32-PICO-MINI microcontroller (Espressif Systems) through I2C or SPI, with on-board Bluetooth Low Energy for wireless transmission. GSR was measured using the on-chip ADC with discrete analog front-end circuitry, and actuator heating was controlled by an N-channel MOSFET (DMN3731U-13, Diodes Incorporated). The full circuit schematic is shown in fig. S43. Power consumption was characterized using a Nordic PPK2 power profiler, with subsystem-level breakdowns shown in fig. S44.
On-body sensing performance characterization
On-body characterization of the electrodes, actuator, and integrated sensors was performed on healthy participants with no history of any ear-related infection and disorders under protocols approved by the California Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 25-1537). Before testing, ear canals were cleaned using non-mechanical over-the-counter ear-cleaning solutions under investigator supervision.
Electrode-ear impedance characterization. Electrode–ear impedance was measured using a CHI760e electrochemical workstation in a three-electrode configuration. The same electrode material was used for the working, reference, and counter electrodes to enable direct comparison among WR-IC-PAAm and commercial electrodes. The working electrode was placed on the actuator in contact with the ear canal, whereas the reference and counter electrodes were positioned around the mastoid region (Fig. 2A). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed with a 5 mV excitation and 2 s quiet time over 1 Hz–1 kHz; for frequencies above 100 Hz, single-frequency mode was used. 
Alpha modulation measurement and analysis. Alpha modulation was evaluated using a standard eyes-open/eyes-closed paradigm. Participants remained relaxed with eyes open for 30 s, followed by 30 s with eyes closed. EEG was recorded at 1 kHz, band-pass filtered from 1 to 50 Hz, and analyzed in LabChart 8 using time–frequency spectrograms and power spectral density (PSD) with a 4k FFT, Welch windowing, 50% overlap, and removal of the zero-frequency component. The AM ratio was defined as the mean alpha-band power (8–12 Hz) during eyes closed (Peyes closed) relative to that during eyes open (Peyes open):

Sleep monitoring using the EarSense
Subject recruitment. Sleep monitoring was conducted in healthy participants and patients with sleep disorders under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the California Institute of Technology (IRB no. 25-1537) and the University of Southern California (IRB no. HS-25-00143). Healthy participants were recruited from the Caltech campus and surrounding communities, while patients were recruited at the USC Sleep Disorders Center following clinical diagnosis by polysomnography (PSG) during their initial evaluation. In total, 20 healthy participants (15 males and 5 females; age 23–34 years) and 7 patients (4 males and 3 females; age 30–47 years) were enrolled. 6 patients underwent simultaneous PSG and EarSense monitoring at the USC Sleep Disorders Center, whereas the remaining participants were studied at the Caltech sleep laboratory using EarSense alone. All participants reported no history of ear-related infection and disorders. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who received a stipend of $200.
Sleep monitoring protocol. Participants were instructed to shower at least 2 h before the study and arrived at the laboratory at least 1 h before their habitual sleep time. Before EarSense placement, participants completed the Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). EarSense and, when applicable, PSG electrodes were then applied following ear-canal and skin preparation. Continuous overnight recording began when participants were ready to sleep, without a fixed sleep duration. Upon awakening, devices were removed and participants completed the SSS, ESS, Functional Outcomes of Sleep Questionnaire (FOSQ), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), followed by a 10-min psychomotor vigilance task (PVT).
Questionnaires for sleep quality evaluation. Subjective sleep outcomes were assessed using the SSS, ESS, FOSQ-30, and PSQI. ESS total scores ranged from 0 to 24 and were categorized into lower normal (0–5), higher normal (6–10), mild excessive (11–12), moderate excessive (13–15), and severe excessive (16–24) daytime sleepiness (45). Global PSQI scores ranged from 0 to 21 and were categorized as good (0–5), average (6–10), poor (11–15), and very poor (16–21) sleep quality (46, 47). FOSQ-30 global scores ranged from 5 to 20, with scores ≤17.9 indicating functional impairment (48). For analysis, scores were categorized into severe (5–12.9), moderate (13–15.9) and mild functional impairment (16–17.9), as well as normal (18–20) functional status. 
PVT test. During the 10-min PVT, participants pressed the spacebar as quickly as possible when a visual stimulus appeared at the center of the screen. Each trial began with a fixation cross followed by the response cue after a randomized delay. Anticipatory responses (RT < 100 ms) and time-out responses (>3000 ms) were excluded. Lapses were defined as RT ≥ 500 ms, and lapse rate was normalized by valid trial count (49, 50). The primary metric used for subsequent analysis was the mean slowest 10% reaction time, which captures the tail of the reaction-time distribution and is sensitive to vigilance lapses.
Sleep stage scoring. For participants undergoing PSG, sleep stages (W, N1, N2, N3, and REM) were scored by a trained sleep technician according to American Academy of Sleep Medicine criteria using Natus SleepWorks software. For EarSense-only recordings, sleep stages were manually scored by a trained technician following standardized home-PSG scoring guidelines.
Sleep EEG correlation and spindle analysis. Sleep EEG correlation between EarSense and PSG (C4–M1), including the overall spectrogram and canonical band-specific spectral profiles (delta to beta), was assessed using multi-taper–processed EEG signals. Sleep spindles were detected during N2 sleep after band-pass filtering within slow (11–13.5 Hz), fast (13.5–16 Hz), and combined (12–16 Hz) spindle bands. Candidate events were identified from the Hilbert-envelope amplitude using thresholds derived from the N2 distribution, with durations constrained to 0.5–2.0 s and gaps <0.2 s merged. 
ML for ECG reconstruction (ECGnet)
Data preprocessing and augmentation. Continuous in-ear composite biopotentials and reference arm ECG were temporally aligned and segmented into overlapping 2-s windows (50% overlap). During training, stochastic augmentation was applied with 50% probability, including random amplitude scaling U(0.8,1.2)and time warping by linear interpolation U(0.9,1.1), followed by random cropping.
ECGnet architecture. ECGnet is a one-dimensional convolutional encoder–attention–decoder network (Fig. 2L; fig. S38). The encoder consisted of two convolutional layers (kernel sizes 15 and 11), each followed by instance normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling (stride = 2). A self-attention block was introduced at the bottleneck to capture long-range dependencies across cardiac cycles. Encoded features were projected into query, key, and value representations using 1×1convolutions, and the resulting attention-enhanced features were fused with the original features through a residual connection with a learnable scaling factor γ. The decoder used two transposed convolution layers (kernel sizes 11 and 15, stride = 2) to reconstruct a single-channel ECG waveform.
Model training, signal reconstruction and post-processing. To preserve spectral and morphological fidelity, ECGnet was trained using a focal frequency loss together with a multi-scale peak loss computed at the original, 2× downsampled, and 4× downsampled resolutions, weighted by 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2, respectively. Spatial weighting based on the normalized absolute temporal gradient of the reference ECG was further applied to emphasize sharp waveform features such as R-peaks.
Continuous ECG signals were reconstructed using a weighted overlap-add strategy with a Hann window and subsequently smoothed using a one-dimensional Gaussian filter (k=31, σ=5.0). Reconstruction performance relative to the reference arm ECG was evaluated using MSE, MAE, Pearson correlation coefficient (r), cosine similarity, SNR, and percentage root mean square difference (PRD), reported as mean ± s.d. with 95% confidence intervals.
ML for affective state analysis
Signal processing and feature extraction. Raw signals were cleaned by removing missing or non-finite samples and identifying recording gaps. EEG and ECG were band-pass filtered from 1 to 50 Hz, and PPG was separated into pulse-related (0.5–5 Hz) and respiration-related (0.1–0.8 Hz) components. GSR, CBT, and IMU were used without additional filtering. Signals were segmented into synchronized non-overlapping windows across modalities, using 30 s epochs for EEG, ECG, PPG, GSR, and CBT, and 5 s windows for IMU. Feature sequences were truncated to the minimum window count across modalities to preserve alignment.
Modality-specific features were extracted from each epoch. EEG features included Hjorth parameters, band powers, band ratios, spectral entropy, mean and peak frequencies, and time-domain statistics. ECG features included heart rate, RR interval statistics, and time- and frequency-domain HRV metrics. PPG features included pulse rate, beat-interval statistics, respiration features, channel-wise statistics, and cross-channel ratios. GSR, CBT, and IMU features included descriptive statistics together with slope, derivative, motion-intensity, and dominant-frequency measures as appropriate.
Model training and interpretation. Labels were derived from session-level PANAS and STAI-Y1 scores and assigned to all epochs within the corresponding session. Constant features were removed using a variance threshold. Features were normalized within each subject using baseline recordings and then z-score standardized using parameters estimated from the training folds only. Models were trained and evaluated by cross-validation, with performance assessed exclusively on held-out folds. 
Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP (Fig4. K and L). Tree-based, linear, and other nonlinear models were analyzed using TreeExplainer, LinearExplainer, and KernelExplainer, respectively. SHAP values were computed on held-out test samples and aggregated across folds to generate global attribution and decision plots.
ML for sleep staging and functional and cognitive performance prediction
Signal processing and contextual input construction. EarSense overnight multimodal recordings in European Data Format were paired with technician-scored hypnograms (Wake, N1, N2, N3, REM), and their timestamps were aligned. Data were segmented into 30s epochs with a sleep-stage label. All signals were resampled to 100 Hz and band-pass filtered (EEG: 0.5–45 Hz; ECG: 0.5–40 Hz; PPG: 0.5–8 Hz; IMU: 0.1–15 Hz). Each epoch was independently z-score normalized, and adjacent epochs were concatenated such that the input for target epoch t was t-1,t,t+1. During training, data augmentation was applied with 70% probability, including random temporal shifts (≤80 samples), amplitude scaling (0.9–1.1), Gaussian jitter (σ=0.01), and random masking of up to two temporal segments (maximum length 250 samples).
Sleep stage classification. Sleep staging was performed using a one-dimensional convolutional neural network with residual connections, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and dilated convolutions. The network consisted of a stem convolution layer (kernel size 11, stride 2) followed by four stages of residual blocks containing parallel convolutional paths (kernel sizes 5 and 7), dropout (0.15), and squeeze-and-excitation recalibration (r=8). Dilation rates increased from 1 to 8, while channel dimensions increased from 32 to 128. Intermediate stages used stride-2 convolutions for temporal downsampling, and final features were aggregated by adaptive average pooling and passed to a two-layer multilayer perceptron classifier.
To address class imbalance and the ambiguity of N1 sleep, a two-stage cascade strategy was used. Stage 1 was trained as a binary classifier to distinguish N1 from all other stages using inverse-square-root weighted sampling. Stage 2 classified non-N1 epochs into Wake, N2, N3, and REM using inverse-frequency sampling. Both stages were optimized with AdamW (learning rate 1×10-3, weight decay 1×10-4) and cosine-annealing scheduling. The loss combined focal loss (γ=2.0) with label smoothing (0.03 for Stage 1; 0.05 for Stage 2). During inference, the Stage 1 N1 probability was nonlinearly down-weighted (α=1.4), and the remaining probability mass was redistributed across the Stage 2 outputs to reconstruct the final five-class probability distribution.
Sleep-derived functional and cognitive performance prediction.Each epoch was converted into a multimodal feature vector containing time- and frequency-domain descriptors. EEG features included summary statistics and canonical band powers. ECG and PPG features included amplitude and variability descriptors together with peak-rate proxies, whereas IMU features were derived from the magnitude of the three-axis acceleration. Epoch-level features were aggregated into subject-level representations using mean, standard deviation, selected quantiles (q10,q50,q90), temporal-instability measures, and coarse sleep-architecture surrogates, and concatenated into the final feature vector zi.
ESS, FOSQ-30, and PSQI outcomes were grouped into discrete categories and predicted using multinomial logistic regression with L2regularization, inverse-frequency class weights, and leave-one-subject-out validation. Next-day vigilance was predicted from ziusing a multilayer perceptron regressor with fully connected layers and ReLU activations, with the mean slowest 10% reaction time as the target. Regression performance was evaluated using MAE, RMSE, and R2. 
Text S1 Physiological and anatomical basis of integrated brain-body sensing from the ear
The human ear provides a unique anatomical and physiological site for integrated multimodal sensing. Conventional physiological monitoring distributes sensors across multiple body locations, such as the scalp for electroencephalography (EEG), the chest for electrocardiography (ECG), peripheral sites for photoplethysmography (PPG), respiration, and temperature. In contrast, the ear’s compact structure and vascular connectivity enable simultaneous access to neural, cardiac, respiratory, thermal, and autonomic signals from a single location. This capability arises from the proximity of the auditory canal to cortical structures, the propagation of cardiac electrical fields through conductive tissues, the rich vascularization of the auricular region, and the thermal coupling between the tympanic membrane and central circulation (Fig. 1A).
The ear canal lies in close proximity to cortical regions that generate prominent EEG rhythms. Compared with scalp recordings, which are affected by hair, skin, and cranial musculature, the ear canal provides a near-hairless conductive pathway that reduces signal attenuation. The temporal and temporoparietal cortices, involved in sensory processing, attention, and sleep regulation, are located adjacent to the auditory canal, allowing neural activity to be detected using in-ear or peri-auricular electrodes (51). The semi-enclosed geometry of the ear canal further reduces environmental interference and mechanical artifacts from facial and jaw movements. (52, 53), enabling reliable detection of canonical EEG features, including alpha and theta rhythms, slow-wave activity, and transient events such as sleep spindles.
Cardiac activity is also detectable at the ear through volume conduction. Electrical activity generated by the heart propagates throughout the body as a time-varying dipole field (54). Blood, as a highly conductive medium, facilitates transmission of these signals along vascular pathways supplying the head. The carotid arteries pass near the ear and branch into vessels supplying the auricular and temporal regions, while the internal carotid artery traverses the temporal bone near the middle and inner ear. These pathways enable cardiac potentials to reach the ear, allowing detection of R-peaks and beat-to-beat timing (55). Although signal amplitudes are smaller than at the chest, they remain sufficient for extracting heart rate and heart-rate variability.
Respiratory dynamics can be inferred from cardiovascular modulation preserved in the auricular vasculature (56). The ear is highly vascularized, providing strong pulsatile signals suitable for PPG (57). Respiratory cycles modulate intrathoracic pressure, influencing venous return, stroke volume, and arterial pressure, which manifest as periodic amplitude variations in PPG signals. Compared with peripheral sites prone to vasoconstriction and motion artifacts, the ear canal maintains relatively stable perfusion, enabling reliable extraction of respiration rate and respiratory-coupled cardiovascular dynamics (58).
The tympanic membrane provides a robust proxy for core body temperature (CBT). It is thin, highly vascularized, and supplied by branches of both the external and internal carotid arteries (59), carrying blood representative of the central circulation. Its direct thermal coupling to cerebral blood flow allows tympanic temperature to closely track brain temperature. The semi-enclosed ear canal minimizes heat exchange with the environment, improving measurement stability compared with skin temperature. These properties underpin the widespread clinical use of tympanic thermometry as a noninvasive indicator of CBT.
The peri-auricular region also enables monitoring of autonomic activity through electrodermal signals. The postauricular skin contains eccrine sweat glands innervated by sympathetic cholinergic fibers (59, 60), allowing changes in autonomic arousal to manifest as measurable variations in skin conductance. This region is relatively protected from airflow and mechanical disturbance, providing a stable site for electrodermal sensing.
Together, these anatomical and physiological features establish the ear as a compact and integrated sensing site where neural, cardiovascular, respiratory, thermal, and autonomic signals converge. This co-localization enables synchronized monitoring of multiple physiological systems from a single wearable interface and supports integrated analysis of brain–body dynamics in applications such as sleep, stress, and cognitive performance.

Text S2 Mechanism of the bilayer PDMS-SMA actuator
The actuation and recovery of a PDMS–SMA bilayer can be described as the temperature-dependent equilibrium of a perfectly bonded composite beam driven by mismatched eigenstrains. Consider a slender bilayer of width b consisting of a PDMS layer of thickness tp bonded to an NiTi SMA sheet of thickness ts. Let z denote the through-thickness coordinate with z=0 at the interface, z>0 within PDMS, and z<0 within SMA. 
Under Euler–Bernoulli kinematics, the axial strain field is assumed linear through thickness,
ε(z)=ε0​+κz                                                              (6.1)
where ε0 is the uniform axial strain of the composite midline and k is the curvature of the bilayer. The mechanical strain that generates stress in each layer is the deviation of εz from that layer’s eigenstrain εi\*(T), which represents the strain the layer would adopt if unconstrained at temperature T.
For PDMS, the dominant eigenstrain comes from the pre-stretch during bonding. Assume the PDMS have original free length L0 and is stretched to λL0 during bonding, the pre-stretch eigenstrain can be approximated as εpre≈λ-1. Including thermal expansion of PDMS, the PDMS eigenstrain can be written as
εp\*(T)=εpre+αp (T-Tbond)                                             (6.2)
where αp is the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of PDMS and Tbond is the bonding temperature. 
For SMA, the eigenstrain contains a transformation component and thermal expansion,
εs\*(T)=εtr(T)+αs (T-Tbond)                                           (6.3)
where εtr(T) is the temperature-dependent transformation eigenstrain associated with the martensite–austenite transition and αs is the SMA CTE. The SMA elastic modulus is also temperature dependent, Es(T), transitioning from a lower martensitic modulus EM to a higher austenitic modulus EA. The PDMS modulus Ep is treated as an effective tangent modulus about the operating strain.
With these definitions, the axial stresses in the two layers can be expressed as,
σpz,T=Epε0+κz-εp\*T,0≤z≤tp                          (6.4)
σs(z,T)=Es(T)(ε0+κz-εs\*(T)),-ts≤z≤0                       (6.5)
Because the actuator is free-standing, the cross-sectional resultants must satisfy net axial force N=0 and net bending moment M=0:
N=b0tpσpdz+-ts0σsdz=0                                           (6.6)
M=b(0tpσpzdz+-ts0σszdzσsz)=0                                       (6.7)
Carrying out these integrations yields two coupled linear conditions for ε0 and κ:
Ep[(ε0-εp\*)tp+12κtp2]+Es(T)[(ε0-εs\*)ts-12κts2]=0                      (6.8)
 Ep[(ε0-εp\*)tp22+κtp33]+Es(T)[-(ε0-εs\*)ts22-κts33]=0                       (6.9)
Solving these equations gives the temperature-dependent equilibrium curvature κ(T) and average axial strain ε0(T). It shows that bending is not controlled by the stress level of one layer alone but by how the eigenstrain mismatch and stiffness contrast shape the self-equilibrated force and moment resultants.
In the room-temperature state, the SMA is predominantly martensitic, so Es(T)≈EM, and εtr(T) is small relative to the large PDMS pre-stretch eigenstrain. The effective mismatch εp\*-εs\* is therefore large, and the equilibrium solution typically yields a substantial curvature. Physically, the structure lowers elastic energy by redistributing the large PDMS pre-stretch through thickness via bending rather than attempting a uniform contraction that would require larger incompatible axial forces. The PDMS is globally still longer than its original L0, so it remains predominantly in tensile stress relative to the L0 reference, while the SMA carries the complementary stress required by N=0 and M=0.
Upon heating, two concurrent SMA effects drive straightening. First, Es(T) increases significantly toward EA, giving the SMA stronger leverage in both force and moment balance. Second, εtr(T) evolves toward the trained austenitic state, and its preferred austenitic length is close to the bonding length, the effective eigenstrain mismatch decreases with temperature. In the equilibrium equations, these changes shift the solution toward smaller ∣κ∣. The hot-state actuator is therefore nearly straight, because the through-thickness stress distribution rearranges to make the net bending moment small while maintaining zero net axial force. Because EA≫Ep, only a very small additional mechanical strain in the SMA is required to balance substantial PDMS-driven internal forces, so the actuator realizes a “near-complete straightening” state with only a small residual curvature. On cooling, Es(T) decreases back toward EM and εtr(T) reverses along a hysteretic path, rebuilding the mismatch and restoring the cold-state curvature. Within this eigenstrain–equilibrium framework, the reversible sequence of bent at low temperature, nearly straight at high temperature, and bent again on cooling emerges naturally from compatibility, temperature-programmable SMA eigenstrain, and the strong modulus contrast between soft PDMS and phase-transforming SMA.

Text S3 Thermal aging of NiTi SMA
Thermal aging of near-equiatomic NiTi SMA at 400 °C for ~8 h induces coupled changes in thermodynamics, microstructure, and functional response that link precipitation chemistry to calorimetric kinetics and lattice geometry. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) shows that before aging the NiTi alloy features austenite start (As) and finish (Af) temperatures on heating of 31 °C and 42.5 °C, and martensite start (Ms) and finish (Mf) temperatures on cooling of 22.9 °C and 8.5 °C. Room-temperature X-ray diffraction (XRD) further indicates that the untreated alloy is dominated by the B2 austenite phase, with a sharp B2(110) reflection at 2𝜃 ≈ 42.4°, and weaker B2(211) and B2(220) peaks at 77.6°and 92.7°, confirming that the alloy remains largely austenitic across the sub-physiological temperature range (< 37 °C).
After aging, the reverse (heating) transformation shifts upward and sharper, with As ≈ 52.8 °C and Af ≈ 54.4 °C. On cooling, the transformation resolves into two steps: a B2 austenite to R intermediate phase transition at Rs ≈ 43.6 °C and Rf ≈ 39.6 °C, followed by an R phase to B19′ martensite transition at Ms ≈18 °C and Mf ≈ 9.7 °C. Notably, the austenitic peak on heating becomes taller and narrower, indicating a more abrupt transformation. Defining the transformed fraction by a scan-rate-corrected DSC signal,
ξ T= 1∆H T0TqT'dT'                                                (7.1)
∆H= 1βTstartTfinishq(T)dT                                                 (7.2)
with q(T) is the baseline-corrected heat flow (power per mass), β=dT/dt is the scan rate, T0 is a reference temperature below onset, and Tstart ,Tfinish bound the transformation peaks. A reduced peak width corresponds to a steeper dξ/Dt, i.e., a faster and more temperature-precise phase evolution. If the integrated peak area increases after aging, this would also imply a larger ΔH, consistent with a strengthened or more cooperative transformation. Such sharpening is expected to translate into more rapid and better-defined curvature or recovery-stress changes during thermally driven actuation.
The upward shift and sharpening of the austenitic transformation after thermal aging are attributed to coherent Ni4Ti3 precipitation during aging, which sequesters Ni and leaves a slightly Ti-richer B2 matrix (26). This compositional change raises transformation temperatures and, together with precipitate coherency strains, stabilizes the R-phase and sharpens the transformation front. Consistently, room-temperature XRD after aging shows a slight left-shift of B2(110) and additional features in the 38–45° window, indicative of R/B19′ signatures superposed on B2, including peaks near 37.77° and 39.22° [B19′(110)], 41.28° [B19′(011)], 42.59° [B19′(020) shoulder on B2(110)], and 43.72° [R(112)], and 44.75° [B19′(111)]. These diffraction changes suggest that, at room temperature, the aged alloy exhibits increased R/B19′ character relative to the predominantly B2 austenitic untreated alloy, implying a lower effective stiffness in the aged state. Mechanical probing tests on the NiTi cantilevers corroborate this interpretation, showing a reduction in bending modulus from 36.5 GPa before aging to 18.2 GPa after aging.

Text S4 Stress-assisted early straightening below the free-strip AS 
Although the aged NiTi SMA shows elevated reverse transformation temperatures in the free state (As ≈ 52.8 °C and Af ≈ 54.4 °C), the bilayer actuator can straighten from its bent stage with a high recovery rate at ~45 °C, a temperature generally considered skin-safe. This seemingly premature recovery arises from the strong stress sensitivity of the reverse transformation when the SMA is mechanically biased by the PDMS layer. In the low-temperature state, the SMA strip sustains a through-thickness stress gradient: the PDMS-adjacent (inner) face is under compression, while the outer face is under tension. Such stress asymmetry is intrinsic to a curved laminate and can be substantial even when the global temperature remains below the nominal free-strip As. The reverse transformation in NiTi follows a Clausius–Clapeyron-type relationship (61, 62), and to first order,
dσdT=C,ΔT≈σC                                                         (8)
where C is a material constant typically on the order of ~6–10 MPa/°C. Under compressive stress (σ<0), the local austenite onset is effectively reduced by ∣σ∣/C, such that an austenitic band can nucleate at the inner face several degrees below the free-strip As measured by DSC (63). This early nucleation is not merely a local phase change; it has system-level mechanical consequences because it increases the local SMA modulus Es(T) as the material shifts toward austenite and simultaneously activates recovery eigenstrain εs*(T). Both effects enhance the SMA’s ability to counteract the PDMS-driven bending moment, thereby reducing the curvature κ. As κ decreases, the tensile bias at the outer NiTi face is relaxed, narrowing the stress asymmetry and lowering the energetic barrier for transformation there. The transformation front can then sweep across the thickness in a cooperative manner, converting a localized, stress-favored nucleation event into rapid macroscopic straightening.
This stress-assisted pathway is further amplified by the aged alloy’s sharpened reverse transformation. A taller, narrower DSC peak implies a steeper transformed-fraction slope dξ/dTover the reverse transition, indicating that once the transformation is triggered, the phase fraction evolves more abruptly with temperature. Consequently, the actuator can exhibit a high recovery rate within a narrow effective temperature interval, enabling near-complete straightening around ~45 °C despite the higher free-strip As. In this sense, the bilayer architecture leverages internal bending stresses to “advance” the functional onset of recovery relative to standard DSC-derived values for an unconstrained NiTi strip.

Upon heating, reverse phase transformation in the SMA generates recovery stress that counteracts PDMS-induced bending and drives the bilayer toward a straight configuration, accompanied by stress redistribution and partial relaxation.

(C) Enlarged DSC curve of the thermally aged SMA, showing a pronounced upward shift and narrowing of the reverse transformation (As ≈ 52.8 °C; Af ≈ 54.4 °C).
